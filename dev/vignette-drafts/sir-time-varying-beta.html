Amortized R(t): behavioral SIR models across all US states

vignette("sir-epidemic") fits a stochastic SIR outbreak with a constant contact rate \(\beta\). Real epidemics do not hold still, and the interesting question is why they move. Control measures, voluntary behavior change, and depleting susceptibles all push transmission around over the course of an outbreak, and different mechanisms leave different fingerprints on the case curve.

This vignette fits three competing models of that mechanism to daily reported cases in all 50 US states plus Washington, DC, over the first 120 days of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic (2020-01-21 to 2020-05-19). Two of them are behavioral-feedback models taken from Gozzi, Perra and Vespignani’s comparative study (2025, PNAS 122(24) e2421993122), where transmission responds to what people can see happening around them. The third is a phenomenological baseline that simply lets \(\beta\) take a different constant value in each of a few time regimes. Each is trained once, amortized over the prior, and then conditioned on 51 different state curves for the price of a forward pass each. That is what makes a three-model, 51-jurisdiction comparison affordable at all.

Along the way the vignette answers the question public-health teams actually care about — how did the effective reproduction number \(R_e(t) = \beta(t) S(t) / (N \gamma)\) evolve? — and shows two pieces of neuralsbi machinery that matter for time-series inference: an embedding_mlp() summary network, and a prior predictive check that catches the kind of model error no amount of training will fix.

The NYT series is cumulative, and it is not monotone: data revisions occasionally push the running total down. We difference it to daily new cases, clipping the revision artifacts at zero.

daily_cases <- function(state_df) {
  full <- data.frame(date = seq(start_date, end_date, by = "day"))
  full <- merge(full, state_df[, c("date", "cases")], by = "date", all.x = TRUE)
  full$cases[is.na(full$cases)] <- 0
  full$cases <- cummax(full$cases)             # enforce a monotone cumulative count
  new <- c(full$cases[1], diff(full$cases))
  new[new < 0] <- 0                            # data-revision artifacts
  new
}

Daily counts carry a strong weekday cycle — fewer tests processed and fewer results reported at weekends — that no SIR model generates and none of these models should be asked to fit. We take a centred 7-day mean as the summary statistic, and the key point is that we will apply the same smoother to simulated data. The network then learns \(p(\theta \mid s(x))\) for a summary \(s\) that means the same thing on both sides; smoothing only the observation would be a fudge, and the simulated and observed data would no longer be comparable.

smooth7 <- function(m) {
  m <- if (is.matrix(m)) m else matrix(m, nrow = 1)
  out <- m
  for (i in seq_len(ncol(m))) {
    idx <- max(1L, i - 3L):min(ncol(m), i + 3L)
    out[, i] <- rowMeans(m[, idx, drop = FALSE])
  }
  out
}

Three mechanisms, one core

All three models share the same discrete-time, binomial-transition SIR core used in vignette("sir-epidemic"): susceptible, infected, and recovered counts update via rbinom(), so the process is genuinely stochastic and there is no tractable likelihood for the reported-case curve. Susceptibles deplete — every infection removes someone from \(S\), and that feedback is what separates the effective reproduction number from the contact rate. Three ingredients are shared by all three models and are worth stating before the mechanisms that differ.

The epidemic starts when it starts. The day the virus arrives, \(t_0\), and the number of infections \(I_0\) that arrive with it are inferred, not fixed. Seeding every state with a couple of infections on 2020-01-21 gets the epidemiology wrong twice over. It is wrong about the world — Washington’s outbreak was seeded in January, West Virginia did not report a case until March 17 — and it is wrong about what a two-infection seed does to a 120-day simulation. A supercritical branching process started from two individuals has a substantial extinction probability and, conditional on survival, a final size spread over orders of magnitude; the timing of takeoff then wanders by weeks. Replicate simulations at a single fixed \(\theta\) differ by a factor of several thousand at the peak. Inferring \((t_0, I_0)\) pins the phase with a parameter instead of leaving it to luck, and it is the more faithful model besides: states were seeded by many independent importations, not by a single index case.

Ascertainment is pinned by seroprevalence, not left free. Only a fraction \(\rho\) of infections became a confirmed case in spring 2020. \(\rho\) is inferred, but with an informative prior, and that is not optional. Reported case counts identify the product \(\rho \times \text{incidence}\), not either factor: a small, well-ascertained epidemic and an enormous, barely-ascertained one produce the same curve. Left to itself the fit takes the second branch, because susceptible depletion is then available to flatten the peak cheaply, which drives \(\rho\) into the low thousandths and the attack rate close to 1. Serology settles it from outside the case data: CDC’s large-scale commercial-lab study put spring-2020 infections at roughly 6 to 24 times reported cases across 10 US sites (Havers et al. 2020, JAMA Internal Medicine), i.e. \(\rho\) between about 0.04 and 0.17. We encode that as a logit-normal prior centered at 0.10 with 95% mass in \([0.05, 0.19]\).

Population size \(N\) is a known covariate. The simulator draws it from a distribution spanning real US state sizes during training, and the estimator conditions on \(\log N\) alongside the case curve, so one trained network generalizes from Wyoming to California. The recovery rate \(\gamma\) is fixed: with only case counts the generation interval is not separately identifiable from \(\beta(t)\), so we set \(1/\gamma = 7\) days, in line with early estimates of the COVID-19 serial interval (Li et al. 2020, NEJM).

Model 1 — piecewise-constant \(\beta(t)\) (the phenomenological baseline)

The first model makes no claim about why transmission changed: it lets \(\log\beta\) take a different constant value in each of a few time regimes, blending between them over a smooth, roughly one-week logistic transition (policy roll-out and behavior change unfold over days, not instantaneously). It is a flexible curve fit, and it is the arm the other two have to beat.

How many regimes, and where do the boundaries go? We answer that from the national curve rather than by assumption, fitting a continuous piecewise-linear regression to log(new7) and selecting breakpoints by BIC.

nat <- national[national$new7 > 0, ]
y <- log(nat$new7); day <- nat$day; n_obs <- length(y)

fit_bic <- function(taus) {
  X <- cbind(1, day, sapply(taus, function(tau) pmax(day - tau, 0)))
  fit <- lm.fit(X, y)
  if (any(is.na(fit$coefficients))) return(NULL)
  rss <- sum(fit$residuals^2); p <- ncol(X)
  list(bic = n_obs * log(rss / n_obs) + p * log(n_obs), taus = taus)
}

cand <- seq(10, 110, by = 5)
best_by_k <- lapply(0:6, function(k) {
  if (k == 0) return(fit_bic(numeric(0)))
  combs <- combn(cand, k); best <- NULL
  for (j in seq_len(ncol(combs))) {
    r <- fit_bic(combs[, j])
    if (!is.null(r) && (is.null(best) || r$bic < best$bic)) best <- r
  }
  best
})

BIC keeps improving out to 6 breakpoints, so raw BIC alone does not pick a winner — a second signal does. Compare the breakpoint sets themselves: going from 2 to 3 breakpoints refines the previous solution ({30, 70} becomes {25, 40, 70}), but going from 3 to 4 does not — {25, 40, 70} is replaced wholesale by {15, 20, 40, 70}, dropping day 25 and inserting two breakpoints only 5 days apart, both inside the window where national counts were in the single or low double digits. That is the signature of a search fitting noise in small counts rather than finding new structure: real inflections give stable refinements as k grows, spurious ones make the whole configuration jump. We take 3 breakpoints — 4 constant segments — as the last stable, well-supported fit.

knot_days <- c(1, sort(best_by_k[[4]]$taus), days)

n_segments <- length(knot_days) - 1L

total_days <- sum(nominal_dur)
transition_scale <- 7 / 4

The breakpoints get wiggle room per state: community transmission did not take hold, and stay-at-home orders did not land, on the same calendar day everywhere. Rather than move each breakpoint independently — which risks two adjacent breakpoints crossing — we put a prior on each regime’s duration and take the breakpoints as cumulative sums, so the regimes tile the window in order for every draw. Only 3 of the 4 durations are free: the simulator rescales them to span the window exactly, so their common scale does nothing. Parameterizing the free directions directly (three log-ratios against the last regime) keeps every coordinate of \(\theta\) identified — a fourth would be pure noise for the flow to model, and SBC would not catch it, because an unidentified coordinate returns its prior and looks perfectly calibrated.

beta_piecewise <- function(log_beta_segments, log_dur_ratio) {
  n <- nrow(log_beta_segments)
  w <- cbind(exp(log_dur_ratio), 1)
  dur <- w / rowSums(w) * total_days
  tau <- knot_days[1] + t(apply(dur, 1, cumsum))[, seq_len(n_segments - 1L), drop = FALSE]
  w_ext <- c(list(matrix(1, n, days)),
             lapply(seq_len(n_segments - 1L), function(k) {
               outer(tau[, k], t_grid, function(a, t) plogis((t - a) / transition_scale))
             }),
             list(matrix(0, n, days)))
  exp(Reduce(`+`, lapply(seq_len(n_segments), function(j) {
    (w_ext[[j]] - w_ext[[j + 1]]) * log_beta_segments[, j]
  })))
}

Models 2 and 3 — behavioral feedback

Gozzi, Perra and Vespignani (2025) compare mechanistic behavioral epidemic models against data-driven ones across nine cities and the first COVID-19 wave. Their two analytical feedback models need no external mobility data — transmission responds to the epidemic’s own reported output — which makes both of them self-contained simulators, exactly what SBI wants.

EFB (effective force of infection). The force of infection is divided by a factor that grows with what people have recently seen and with what they have seen cumulatively: \[ \lambda(t) = \beta_0 \frac{I(t)}{N} \cdot \frac{1}{1 + \xi\, c(t-1) + \psi \sum_{t' < t} c(t')} . \] \(\xi\) is reactivity to the current news, \(\psi\) to accumulated experience. Six parameters in total.

CBF (compartmental behavioral feedback). Susceptibles move into a risk-averse compartment \(S_B\) at rate \(\beta_B (1 - e^{-g\,c(t-1)})\), drift back at rate \(\mu_B\), and while in \(S_B\) their force of infection is scaled by \(r < 1\). Behavior here is a state of the population that takes time to build and to decay, rather than an instantaneous function of the news. Eight parameters.

Two adaptations to the paper’s specification, both forced by what we are doing here. The paper drives behavior off reported deaths; we drive it off reported cases, which is the only outcome this model generates and was itself a headline number in spring 2020. And we express the driver \(c(t)\) per 100,000 people rather than as a raw count, because a single amortized posterior has to serve Wyoming and California: behavior responds to perceived local risk, which scales with per-capita incidence. With a raw-count driver the same \(\xi\) would mean drastically different behavior in a small state and a large one, and no single \(\xi\) could fit both.

draw_N <- function(n, N_fixed) {
  if (is.null(N_fixed)) exp(stats::runif(n, log(5e5), log(4e7))) else rep(N_fixed, n)
}

# x = log(N) followed by the 7-day-smoothed daily reported cases, log1p'd.
pack <- function(N, reported, S_frac, beta_eff, full) {
  x <- cbind(log(N), log1p(smooth7(reported)))
  if (!full) return(x)
  list(x = x, reported = reported, S_frac = S_frac, beta_eff = beta_eff)
}

# shared: introduce the epidemic at t0 with a Poisson(seed_mean) seed
seed_step <- function(t, t0, seed_mean, S, I, seeded) {
  fresh <- !seeded & (t >= t0)
  if (any(fresh)) {
    s0 <- pmin(stats::rpois(sum(fresh), seed_mean[fresh]), S[fresh])
    I[fresh] <- I[fresh] + s0
    S[fresh] <- S[fresh] - s0
    seeded[fresh] <- TRUE
  }
  list(S = S, I = I, seeded = seeded)
}
sim_pw <- function(theta, N_fixed = NULL, full = FALSE) {
  theta <- matrix(as.numeric(theta), ncol = 10L)
  n <- nrow(theta)
  beta_t <- beta_piecewise(theta[, 1:4, drop = FALSE], theta[, 5:7, drop = FALSE])
  t0 <- 1 + (days - 1) * plogis(theta[, 8])
  seed_mean <- exp(theta[, 9]); rho <- plogis(theta[, 10])
  N <- draw_N(n, N_fixed)

  S <- N; I <- rep(0, n); seeded <- rep(FALSE, n)
  reported <- matrix(0, n, days); S_frac <- matrix(0, n, days)
  n_sub <- 2L; dt <- 1 / n_sub; p_rec <- 1 - exp(-gamma_fixed * dt)
  for (t in t_grid) {
    st <- seed_step(t, t0, seed_mean, S, I, seeded); S <- st$S; I <- st$I; seeded <- st$seeded
    H <- rep(0, n)
    for (s in seq_len(n_sub)) {
      p_inf <- pmin(pmax(1 - exp(-beta_t[, t] * I / N * dt), 0), 1)
      dSI <- stats::rbinom(n, pmax(round(S), 0), p_inf)
      dIR <- stats::rbinom(n, pmax(round(I), 0), p_rec)
      S <- S - dSI; I <- I + dSI - dIR; H <- H + dSI    # susceptibles deplete
    }
    S_frac[, t] <- S / N
    reported[, t] <- stats::rbinom(n, pmax(round(H), 0), rho)
  }
  pack(N, reported, S_frac, beta_t, full)
}

sim_efb <- function(theta, N_fixed = NULL, full = FALSE) {
  theta <- matrix(as.numeric(theta), ncol = 6L)
  n <- nrow(theta)
  beta0 <- exp(theta[, 1]); xi <- exp(theta[, 2]); psi <- exp(theta[, 3])
  t0 <- 1 + (days - 1) * plogis(theta[, 4])
  seed_mean <- exp(theta[, 5]); rho <- plogis(theta[, 6])
  N <- draw_N(n, N_fixed)

  S <- N; I <- rep(0, n); seeded <- rep(FALSE, n)
  c_prev <- rep(0, n); C_cum <- rep(0, n)
  reported <- matrix(0, n, days); S_frac <- matrix(0, n, days)
  beta_eff <- matrix(0, n, days)
  n_sub <- 2L; dt <- 1 / n_sub; p_rec <- 1 - exp(-gamma_fixed * dt)
  for (t in t_grid) {
    st <- seed_step(t, t0, seed_mean, S, I, seeded); S <- st$S; I <- st$I; seeded <- st$seeded
    bt <- beta0 / (1 + xi * c_prev + psi * C_cum)      # the behavioral response
    beta_eff[, t] <- bt
    H <- rep(0, n)
    for (s in seq_len(n_sub)) {
      p_inf <- pmin(pmax(1 - exp(-bt * I / N * dt), 0), 1)
      dSI <- stats::rbinom(n, pmax(round(S), 0), p_inf)
      dIR <- stats::rbinom(n, pmax(round(I), 0), p_rec)
      S <- S - dSI; I <- I + dSI - dIR; H <- H + dSI
    }
    S_frac[, t] <- S / N
    rep_t <- stats::rbinom(n, pmax(round(H), 0), rho)
    reported[, t] <- rep_t
    c_prev <- rep_t / N * PER                          # per 100k, so one xi fits every state
    C_cum <- C_cum + c_prev
  }
  pack(N, reported, S_frac, beta_eff, full)
}

sim_cbf <- function(theta, N_fixed = NULL, full = FALSE) {
  theta <- matrix(as.numeric(theta), ncol = 8L)
  n <- nrow(theta)
  beta0 <- exp(theta[, 1]); betaB <- exp(theta[, 2]); g <- exp(theta[, 3])
  r <- plogis(theta[, 4]); muB <- exp(theta[, 5])
  t0 <- 1 + (days - 1) * plogis(theta[, 6])
  seed_mean <- exp(theta[, 7]); rho <- plogis(theta[, 8])
  N <- draw_N(n, N_fixed)

  S <- N; SB <- rep(0, n); I <- rep(0, n); seeded <- rep(FALSE, n)
  c_prev <- rep(0, n)
  reported <- matrix(0, n, days); S_frac <- matrix(0, n, days)
  beta_eff <- matrix(0, n, days)
  n_sub <- 2L; dt <- 1 / n_sub; p_rec <- 1 - exp(-gamma_fixed * dt)
  for (t in t_grid) {
    st <- seed_step(t, t0, seed_mean, S, I, seeded); S <- st$S; I <- st$I; seeded <- st$seeded
    pB <- betaB * (1 - exp(-g * c_prev))               # awareness-driven uptake
    H <- rep(0, n)
    for (s in seq_len(n_sub)) {
      lam <- beta0 * I / N
      # a susceptible faces competing risks -- infection or adopting safer
      # behavior -- so draw the total departures and split them, rather than
      # letting whichever event is applied first pre-empt the other
      tot_haz <- lam + pB
      dS <- stats::rbinom(n, pmax(round(S), 0),
                          pmin(pmax(1 - exp(-tot_haz * dt), 0), 1))
      dSI <- stats::rbinom(n, dS, ifelse(tot_haz > 0, lam / tot_haz, 0))
      dSB <- dS - dSI
      dBI <- stats::rbinom(n, pmax(round(SB), 0),
                           pmin(pmax(1 - exp(-r * lam * dt), 0), 1))
      dBS <- stats::rbinom(n, pmax(round(SB) - dBI, 0),
                           pmin(pmax(1 - exp(-muB * dt), 0), 1))
      dIR <- stats::rbinom(n, pmax(round(I), 0), p_rec)
      S <- S - dS + dBS; SB <- SB + dSB - dBI - dBS
      I <- I + dSI + dBI - dIR; H <- H + dSI + dBI
    }
    S_frac[, t] <- (S + SB) / N
    # transmission rate averaged over the susceptible pool, for reporting R_e
    beta_eff[, t] <- beta0 * (S + r * SB) / pmax(S + SB, 1)
    rep_t <- stats::rbinom(n, pmax(round(H), 0), rho)
    reported[, t] <- rep_t
    c_prev <- rep_t / N * PER
  }
  pack(N, reported, S_frac, beta_eff, full)
}

The priors. Every parameter is unconstrained on \(\mathbb{R}\) — the log/logit transforms mean a single Gaussian prior with no truncated support, so the posterior needs no rejection sampling for leakage. Naming each mean vector carries the names through every posterior draw, SBC result, and diagnostic plot. Note that \(\beta_0\) means different things in the two families: in the piecewise model it is an effective rate per regime, so its prior is centred on \(R = 2\) and deliberately wide in both directions; in the behavioral models it is the intrinsic transmission rate with the decline explained mechanistically, so it is centred on \(R_0 = 3\), the wild-type value.

Summarizing 120 days with an embedding network

The observation is 121 numbers: \(\log N\) plus a 120-day case curve. Feeding that straight into the flow wastes capacity — the flow would have to learn, from the conditioning path alone, that neighboring days are nearly redundant. The alternative most tutorials reach for is to hand-pick summary statistics (bin the curve into weeks, take a growth rate and a peak height), which throws away whatever the hand-picked set failed to capture.

embedding_mlp() does the compression by learning it. It is a small MLP mapping the standardized 121-dimensional observation to output_dim features, trained jointly with the density estimator, so the features are optimized for exactly one thing: predicting \(\theta\). The estimator then conditions on those features rather than on the raw curve.

Check the prior predictive before training anything

Amortized NPE learns \(p(\theta \mid x)\) over the prior predictive distribution of \(x\). Data that the prior predictive essentially never produces sit in a region where the trained network is extrapolating, and its posterior there is not to be trusted — no amount of training fixes that. So before spending a simulation budget, check that each model can produce curves like the ones we intend to condition on.

set.seed(11)
n_pp <- 1500
sims <- list(PW = list(sim = sim_pw, prior = prior_pw),
             EFB = list(sim = sim_efb, prior = prior_efb),
             CBF = list(sim = sim_cbf, prior = prior_cbf))

pp_check <- function(m, state) {
  theta <- sample_prior(m$prior, n_pp)
  lx <- m$sim(theta, N_fixed = pop[[state]])[, -1, drop = FALSE]   # already log1p
  d <- rowMeans(abs(lx - rep(log1p(case_daily_s[state, ]), each = n_pp)))
  mean(d < 1)
}
round(sapply(sims, function(m) sapply(c("New York", "Michigan", "West Virginia"),

The number to watch is what fraction of prior draws land within a factor of \(e\) of the observed curve, averaged over the 120 days. It is small — these priors are deliberately vague — but it is not a rounding error, so the observations sit inside the bulk of each prior predictive rather than out in its tail. Fixing the seeding day at day 1 instead of inferring it fails exactly this check: West Virginia’s seven empty weeks can then only be produced by a sub-threshold contact rate, which usually means extinction, so the observed curve drops out of the band’s lower edge.

Validate before trusting a single curve

Simulation-based calibration (SBC) checks that each posterior’s credible intervals have the coverage they claim, using fresh prior draws the network never saw during training. For each of many simulated “true” parameter values we compute where that value ranks among posterior draws conditioned on the resulting simulated data; if the posterior is calibrated the rank is equally likely to fall anywhere, so the rank histogram should come out flat. Skew toward the edges means overconfidence; skew toward the middle, underconfidence.

Posterior predictive: daily incidence, three models

Now the comparison that matters. For each state we condition each trained posterior on that state’s own daily curve and population, push the draws back through the simulator, and compare the predicted daily incidence to what was reported.

We summarize the predictive by its median, not its mean. Daily counts here span four orders of magnitude and the estimator is trained on \(\log(1+x)\); the predictive distribution on each day is strongly right-skewed, so its mean tracks the upper tail rather than the center. Plotting the mean of a skewed count predictive makes a well-calibrated fit look like a systematic overshoot.

The three bands differ strikingly in width — the piecewise model’s is an order of magnitude wider at New York’s peak than either behavioral model’s. It is tempting to read the narrow bands as overconfidence. They are not, and the way to tell is to hold \(\theta\) fixed and re-simulate: whatever spread survives is the simulator’s own process noise, with no posterior uncertainty in it at all.

# One representative theta per model (the coordinatewise posterior median --
# not necessarily a high-density point for a correlated posterior, but it
# produces a New-York-like epidemic, which is all this diagnostic needs).
noise_at_fixed_theta <- function(nm, state = "New York", n_rep = 300) {
  draws <- sample(posterior(fits[[nm]], x_obs = obs_for(state)), 500)
  one <- apply(draws, 2, stats::median)
  rep_theta <- matrix(rep(one, n_rep), nrow = n_rep, byrow = TRUE)
  r <- sims[[nm]]$sim(rep_theta, N_fixed = pop[[state]], full = TRUE)
  pred <- smooth7(r$reported)
  j <- which.max(case_daily_s[state, ])
  c(peak_cv = stats::sd(pred[, j]) / mean(pred[, j]),
    peak_day_sd = stats::sd(apply(pred, 1, which.max)))
}

The open-loop piecewise model has roughly an order of magnitude more peak-height variability at fixed \(\theta\) than either behavioral model. That is the feedback doing exactly what feedback does: run hot and \(\beta\) falls, run cold and it recovers, so the loop damps the demographic noise that an open-loop model lets through. A behavioral epidemic model is therefore intrinsically more predictable than a phenomenological one at the same parameter values, and most of the narrow predictive band is mechanism rather than posterior. Whether the remaining width is honest is a question only SBC can answer — which is why the rank histograms above come before this plot, not after.

R(t) for every state

The effective reproduction number is \[ R_e(t) \;=\; \frac{\beta(t)}{\gamma} \cdot \frac{S(t)}{N}, \] the average number of secondary cases per infection given the susceptibles still available. The \(S(t)/N\) factor is not decoration: in New York the model infers that a substantial share of the population had been infected by mid-May, so \(R_e\) there ends the window well below what the transmission rate alone would suggest. We compute both factors from the simulator’s own state trajectories rather than re-deriving \(\beta(t)\) alongside it, so the reported quantity and the fitted model cannot drift apart. For the CBF model \(\beta(t)\) is the transmission rate averaged over the susceptible pool, which mixes the unprotected and risk-averse compartments.

The model used below is whichever scored best above, not a fixed choice. The three are close enough that the winner can move with the simulation budget, so if you re-run this article with a different n_sim do not be surprised to see a different name here.

best <- as.character(scores$model[which.min(scores$rmse_med)])

rt_state <- function(state, nm = best, n_draws = 400) {

\(R_e(t)\) is flat and uninformative before a state’s inferred introduction day — no epidemic, nothing in the data to constrain transmission, so the posterior there is close to the prior — then climbs through early-to-mid March as community transmission spreads, and comes back down toward and below 1 by mid-to-late April, with real heterogeneity in timing and magnitude that a single national estimate would have hidden.

What this comparison bought

The alternative to amortized NPE is fitting each model to each state separately — 153 particle filters or MCMC chains, 153 opportunities for one to fail to converge. Here, training happened three times (1.5 × 104 simulations each), and every state’s posterior under every model cost a forward pass.

Three lessons generalize past this dataset.

Do not leave the epidemic’s phase to demographic noise. A branching process started from a couple of infections and run for four months has a final size spread across orders of magnitude and a takeoff time that wanders by weeks. If nothing in \(\theta\) can absorb that, the posterior predictive integrates over it, and its mean sits far above the data while its 5th percentile sits at zero. Inferring \((t_0, I_0)\) fixes it, and the recovered introduction days are a free check on the change: the posterior puts New York’s near March 1 and West Virginia’s in mid-March, matching when those states actually reported their first cases.

Do not let case counts alone set the epidemic’s scale. They identify \(\rho \times \text{incidence}\). Without external information on \(\rho\) the fit slides along that ridge toward vast, barely-ascertained epidemics, because susceptible depletion is then available to flatten the peak. Seroprevalence data break the tie, and they belong in the prior.

A mechanism can be cheaper than a curve fit. The piecewise model needs 10 parameters to bend \(\beta(t)\) into the right shape and, having no mechanism, happily reports transmission rates for regimes that ended before the state’s epidemic began. The behavioral models get comparable fits from 6 and 8 parameters and put a physical interpretation on every one of them.

One structural difference between the two behavioral models deserves stating, because it is invisible in a 120-day first-wave window and decisive outside one. EFB’s denominator contains the cumulative case count, which only ever grows, so its \(\beta(t)\) is monotone non-increasing: the model can bend an epidemic down but can never let it come back. CBF can, because \(\mu_B\) lets risk-averse individuals drift back into the susceptible pool once incidence falls. Over the first wave the two look similar; over a second wave they could not, and this is the likeliest reason the original study found CBF the better forecaster.

The models still carry real simplifications, worth stating plainly. \(\gamma\) is fixed rather than inferred, so sbc() above is calibrated conditional on that choice, not unconditionally. A single ascertainment rate \(\rho\) stands in for what was, in practice, a patchwork of state and local testing capacity that grew steeply over exactly this window; a time-varying \(\rho(t)\) is the obvious next refinement, at the cost of a parameter confounded with transmission. Each state gets one introduction, whereas states with large international airports saw a trickle of imported cases for weeks before community transmission established — California, whose first weeks carry a handful of traveler-associated cases, is the state every model here fits worst. The behavioral models assume the whole state reacts to a single statewide case count, which is a strong assumption for California and a mild one for Rhode Island. And Louisiana’s Mardi Gras superspreading produced a spike that a smooth behavioral response cannot reproduce. Loosening any of these is a change to the simulator, not to the inference machinery around it — the same amortized-NPE workflow applies unchanged.
